Title: Building and maintaining a healthy raised-bed vegetable garden

A raised-bed vegetable garden gives more control over soil quality than
planting directly in native ground, which matters most in the first two
growing seasons while soil biology is still establishing. The single
highest-leverage decision is soil mix composition: a blend of roughly
40% high-quality compost, 40% topsoil, and 20% coarse material such as
perlite or coarse sand drains well while still holding enough moisture
between waterings. Beds that are too compost-heavy stay soggy and starve
roots of oxygen; beds that are too sandy dry out within a day in hot
weather and require constant attention.

Bed depth affects which crops thrive. Shallow beds (20-25 cm) support
lettuce, herbs, and most leafy greens comfortably. Root vegetables such
as carrots and parsnips need at least 30 cm of loose, stone-free soil to
grow straight; in shallower beds they fork or stay stunted. Tomatoes and
peppers benefit from 30-40 cm depth because their root systems spread
wide as well as deep, and a deeper bed also buffers soil temperature
swings that can stress fruiting plants.

Watering consistency matters more than total water volume. Vegetables
that receive irregular watering -- long dry spells followed by heavy
soaking -- are far more prone to blossom-end rot in tomatoes and
splitting in root vegetables than plants watered on a steady schedule.
A simple test is pushing a finger two knuckles deep into the soil: if it
is dry at that depth, water; if still moist, wait. Mulching the surface
with straw or shredded leaves reduces how often this becomes necessary
by slowing evaporation.

Crop rotation across seasons within the same raised bed reduces pest and
disease buildup. Planting the same family of crop (for example, tomatoes
and peppers, which are both nightshades) in the same bed year after year
lets soil-borne pathogens specific to that family accumulate. Rotating
to an unrelated family, such as legumes or leafy greens, for at least one
season breaks that cycle and, in the case of legumes, also adds nitrogen
back into the soil through root nodules as the plants are cut down at
the end of the season rather than pulled out.
