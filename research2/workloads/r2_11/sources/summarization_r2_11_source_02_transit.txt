Title: Why urban bus schedules drift and what cities do about it

Published bus schedules assume a fixed travel time between stops, but
actual travel time varies with traffic, passenger boarding volume, and
driver behavior, so buses tend to drift away from the printed timetable
as a route progresses. This drift is not random: it is dominated by a
small number of structural causes, and transit agencies that understand
those causes can design schedules and operational rules that keep
service closer to on-time without adding more buses.

The largest single cause is bunching: two buses on the same route that
were originally spaced evenly start to clump together. A bus running
slightly behind schedule picks up more waiting passengers at each stop
(because more people have accumulated), which slows it down further and
makes it later still. Meanwhile the bus behind it finds fewer passengers
waiting, because they already boarded the delayed bus ahead, so the
trailing bus speeds up and catches up. Left uncorrected, this produces
two buses arriving together followed by a long gap, rather than evenly
spaced service.

Signal priority is a secondary but still significant factor. Routes that
pass through many signalized intersections without any priority system
accumulate variable delay at each light, and that variability compounds
across a route with dozens of intersections. Cities that give buses even
limited signal priority -- extending a green light slightly when a bus
is detected approaching -- measurably reduce both average delay and the
variance in arrival time, which matters more to riders than the average
alone, since riders plan around the worst case they have experienced.

Operational countermeasures used by well-run systems include: holding a
leading bus briefly at designated control points if it is running early
relative to the bus behind it, short-turning a badly delayed bus before
the end of the route so it can start its next trip on time rather than
arriving very late, and publishing real-time arrival predictions so
riders can adjust their own arrival at the stop instead of experiencing
the drift as an unexplained wait.
