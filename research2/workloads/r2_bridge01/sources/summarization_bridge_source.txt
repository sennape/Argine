Title: Practical notes on choosing and maintaining a bicycle for daily commuting

A bicycle used for daily commuting has different priorities than one used mainly
for recreation or racing. The three factors that matter most for a commuter are
reliability, comfort over repeated short trips, and low ongoing maintenance effort,
roughly in that order. A bike that requires frequent adjustment or that is
uncomfortable after twenty minutes will quietly stop getting used, regardless of
how capable it is on paper.

Frame geometry affects comfort directly. An upright riding position, achieved
through a higher handlebar relative to the saddle, reduces strain on the wrists,
shoulders, and lower back during short, frequent rides in street clothes. A more
aggressive, low handlebar position is better suited to speed over long distances
but is less forgiving for someone who commutes in work clothing and stops
frequently at intersections.

Drivetrain choice is a tradeoff between simplicity and range. A single-speed or
internally geared hub drivetrain has far fewer exposed, wear-prone parts than a
multi-speed derailleur system, and tolerates rain and road grit with less frequent
cleaning. A derailleur drivetrain offers a wider range of gears for hilly commutes
but needs periodic cable and derailleur-hanger adjustment, especially after the
bike is bumped or transported.

Tires are the single most common source of commuting delays. Narrow, high-pressure
tires roll faster but puncture more easily on debris-strewn urban streets; wider
tires with a puncture-resistant belt roll slightly slower but dramatically reduce
mid-commute flat tires. Keeping tires at the pressure printed on the sidewall,
checked roughly every two weeks, is the single cheapest way to reduce both
punctures and rolling resistance.

Basic maintenance that materially affects reliability includes: lubricating the
chain every one to two weeks of commuting (more often in wet weather), keeping
brake pads replaced before they wear down to the metal backing, and checking that
both wheels spin freely without rubbing against the brake pads. Weather protection
such as fenders and a chain guard reduces the frequency of all of the above, at a
small cost in weight and a small increase in purchase price.
